---
title: Low Water
pov: Dora Penhallow
---

The tide table hung on a nail inside the wheelhouse door, a printed card
from the river authority with a year of high and low water on it in
type so small she needed her father's reading glasses. She found
Tuesday and ran her finger along the line.

Low water, 23:52.

She sat down on the bench. At low water the Cale drew back off the
Saltney mud until the chain lay bare across the bottom of the channel,
and the ferry sat on its keel until the flood lifted it again. Twelve
minutes before low water there was not enough river under the hull to
float a rowing boat, let alone a ferry with a lorry on it.

Nobody had crossed at twenty to midnight on Tuesday. Nobody could have.

She thought about Lusk under his window, telling her the log was the
log. She thought about how many times she had let things go: the late
fuel delivery he signed for and blamed on her, the safety rail he said
he had ordered and never had, the lifebelt that went missing in the
spring. Each time she had told herself it was easier to fix it than to
argue. Her father had done the same, for thirty years, and she had
watched him do it.

The ferry, licensed for twenty and carrying none, rocked once on its
lines as the flood came in under it.

She took the tide card off its nail and put it in her coat with the
log. Then she went into the wheelhouse and took a pencil from the jar,
one of her own, chewed at the end, and wrote under Tuesday's last line
in her square capitals: No crossing possible. Low water 23:52. D.
Penhallow.

It would not stand as evidence. She knew that. But it was in her hand,
and it said what she had seen.

The river inspector came down from the county office on Thursdays and
took his lunch at the pub by the bridge. She had never once spoken to
him without Lusk in the room. She checked the date on her phone. It was
Wednesday.

She ran the evening crossings as usual, all four of them, and wrote
each one in the log in her own hand, upright, and at the end of the last
one she did not put the log back in its tin.
